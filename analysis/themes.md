### Guardrails people own

**Agents are trusted through guardrails that people own (rules that check their output, scoped access, and a firewall on what goes in and what they do) rather than by people reading what they produce.**

Ankit Jain mines years of review comments into rules with owners and turns some into deterministic checks, so people own the rules instead of the code. Karan Vaidya gives agents broad but granular access to each app and puts a firewall on both their inputs and their actions. Utkarsh Shrivastava has Netflix's domain teams run a regression eval suite in CI before any new connector or model change, and scales the gate with the stakes, from reads to irreversible writes. All three replace line-by-line supervision with controls a named person or team maintains. Mihail Eric goes further for vertical AI: there is no absolute correctness, so in-house domain experts define what good looks like and evaluations follow them. Lauren Tan puts the most weight on it: on the Grok Bot team every correction to an agent becomes a lint rule, a banned API or a better design, prose rules come last, and code review has shrunk to reading verification artifacts (videos, screenshots, model checks), which is what lets hundreds of pull requests a day land. Overcut gives each narrow agent only the tools and permissions it needs, adds human approval gates where the team wants them, and keeps a full audit trail. Mixedbread carries each document's access rules into the index and makes every search name its user. Ramp attaches the whole agent session to a pull request, so a reviewer checks evidence instead of reading the diff. Steve Yegge's counter-point: an agent yelled at with too many rules fences itself in until nothing gets done. On Nov 12, Michael Liendo (Auth0) scales human-in-the-loop with the stakes: scoped, short-lived credentials from a token store, step-up permissions when a task needs more, out-of-band CIBA approval for high-stakes actions, and virtual cards with limits instead of the real card. Walden Yan's Cognition has agents test in local VMs and send back videos as proof, and Arsalan Bashir (Sentry) gives the agent a cheap deterministic check, such as a dry-run query, so it can iterate and users can trust the result.

### Context for AI

**AI does its best work on context people have already curated (the intent captured in a session, modeled data, a map of the database), not on raw systems.**

### Decision load

**As agents take over the work, the decisions left to people become the scarce, tiring part, so teams record them, queue them for a person, and keep them few enough to make well.**

Across the talks, agents do more of the doing and people are left with the deciding. Ankit Jain finds that human review comments were mostly about decisions and has agents argue so the decisions surface and get written down. Karan Vaidya's security agents queue uncertain findings for a person to judge in the morning. Scott Tolinski's survey shows the cost: running many agents multiplies the decisions and wears people out, and fewer agents in parallel helps. Michelle Chen argues the other way: Cloudflare's Clef decision model lets an agent make the call itself, without minutes of LLM reasoning or a human in the loop, so it can run around the clock. Steve Yegge finds the more agents he deploys, the more humans he needs, and Simon Willison that the job feels harder because the easy part is handled: it doesn't get easier, you just get faster. Cognition's Android-parity agent ran 44 days with almost no human messages, escalating only the key decisions.

### Faster tokens
